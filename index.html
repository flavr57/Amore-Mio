Private Credit Comes to Ordinary Investors: The Case for Interval Funds
Interval funds are registered investment funds — cousins of mutual funds — that invest in private credit: direct loans made to mid-market companies that can't or don't want to access public bond markets. Unlike a standard mutual fund you can redeem any day, interval funds offer liquidity windows only periodically, typically quarterly, and usually cap redemptions at around 5% of fund assets per window. Private credit strategies housed in these vehicles have historically delivered yields in a rough range of 8–12%, though that figure varies considerably by manager quality, vintage year, and the creditworthiness of underlying borrowers — treat any such range as a conversation starter, not a forecast. The mechanism is direct: you're lending money to operating businesses and earning interest over time, capturing a spread above public fixed-income in exchange for giving up daily liquidity. The honest trade-off is exactly that: limited liquidity is structural, not incidental — interval funds are the wrong tool if you might need the capital back on short notice. To dig deeper, compare the prospectuses and distribution histories of offerings from Blue Owl Capital, Ares Management, and Blackstone's BCRED — each publishes quarterly fact sheets with fee breakdowns and historical distribution data worth reading side by side. How to Coax Your Orchid Into a Fresh Bloom Cycle This Winter
November's shortening days are your orchid's signal to rest — but you can use that rest period to set up a spectacular spring bloom. Move your Phalaenopsis to a spot where nighttime temperatures run about 10–15°F cooler than the daytime (a bright windowsill away from heating vents works beautifully), and reduce watering to every ten days or so, letting the mix nearly dry between sessions. This gentle cool-down mimics the highland climate orchids evolved in, and reliably triggers a new flower spike within six to ten weeks.

Sow Now, Bloom Bigger Later: Getting the Best From Your Seeds
November is an underrated month for sowing cold-hardy annuals and perennials that benefit from cool stratification — the process by which seeds are primed to germinate by naturally mimicking winter conditions. Horticulturist Sally Nex recommends pre-soaking hard-coated seeds for 12–24 hours, using fresh seed compost rather than garden soil, and labeling everything immediately, since memory and seedling trays are equally unreliable. Getting these basics right now dramatically improves germination rates and gives plants a meaningful head start come spring.
