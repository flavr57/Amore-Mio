The Case for Letting Munis Do the Quiet Work
Municipal bond funds like VTEB (Vanguard Tax-Exempt Bond ETF) and MUB (iShares National Muni Bond ETF) hold diversified baskets of bonds issued by states, cities, and public agencies — and the interest they pass along to investors is generally exempt from federal income tax. Historically, depending on the fund's credit quality and average duration, muni yields have run in a range that looks modest at first glance but often competes well with taxable bonds once you factor in what you'd otherwise send to the IRS. The mechanism is clean: municipalities borrow from investors to fund roads, schools, and hospitals, and Congress has long allowed that interest to flow to holders free of federal tax, partly to help governments borrow at lower rates. The honest trade-off is that munis yield less in absolute terms than comparable taxable bonds, so the advantage depends almost entirely on your marginal tax rate — the math narrows fast if your bracket is low. Tax law around the federal exemption has been stable for a long time, but your personal tax picture changes, and a CPA can confirm whether the after-tax comparison actually favors munis in your situation before you act on it. A good research thread: pull the fund detail pages for both VTEB at Vanguard and MUB at iShares, compare their expense ratios, average durations, and credit quality breakdowns side by side, then run the tax-equivalent yield formula — stated yield divided by (1 minus your marginal rate) — to see how they stack up against a taxable bond fund you already own or are considering. Tomorrow the story gets more interesting: state-specific muni funds that may offer a second layer of tax exemption — worth knowing if you're in a high-tax state.

In Southern California, October often brings one final wave of color from bougainvillea before cooler nights begin to slow things down — it's worth letting the plant finish that cycle fully before any significant pruning.
