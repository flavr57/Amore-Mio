How a Treasury Ladder Turns Government Debt Into Steady Income
A Treasury ladder is a hands-on income strategy in which you buy individual U.S. Treasury bills, notes, or bonds with staggered maturity dates — tranches coming due every three, six, nine, or twelve months, for example — so that cash arrives in your account on a rolling, predictable schedule rather than all at once. Yields across the ladder have historically ranged from under 2% in the post-2008 low-rate era to roughly 4–5% or higher during tighter monetary periods, so what you actually earn depends heavily on the rate environment when you build it. The mechanism is straightforward: you lend directly to the federal government, collect interest along the way, and when each rung matures you either spend the principal or roll it into a new rung at the far end of the ladder, extending your income stream indefinitely. The honest trade-off is reinvestment risk — when a rung matures in a lower-rate environment, you'll be reinvesting at softer yields, and if you need money before maturity, Treasuries can be sold on the secondary market but not always at the price you paid. A good research thread to pull: visit TreasuryDirect.gov to browse current auction calendars, then compare the DIY experience against the fixed-income ladder-building tools offered by Fidelity and Vanguard, which let you model rungs side by side. Tomorrow we turn to TIPS and inflation-protected Treasury funds like SCHP and VTIP — the natural companion to a ladder when you want your income to keep pace with rising prices.
