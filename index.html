Make the Tax Code Work Harder: Bunching Gifts Through a Donor-Advised Fund
Here's the core idea: every year the IRS lets you choose between taking a flat standard deduction or tallying up actual deductible expenses — mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable gifts — and itemizing if that total runs higher. The bunching strategy exists because the standard deduction has generally been high enough since 2018 that most households never clear the bar in any given year, meaning those individual charitable gifts feel generous but never actually reduce your tax bill. A donor-advised fund (DAF) — think Fidelity Charitable, Schwab Charitable, or Vanguard Charitable — solves this by letting you front-load several years' worth of giving into one large contribution, claim the full deduction in that filing year, and then distribute grants to your chosen nonprofits over time at whatever pace you like. For a self-employed business owner already running Schedule C deductions, stacking a big DAF contribution in a high-income year can push you comfortably over the standard deduction threshold, sheltering what might otherwise be a hefty bill — historically, households that bunch effectively can shelter tens of thousands in a single filing. The honest trade-off is that once assets go into a DAF they can only flow to qualified charities, never back to you, and since these rules have shifted before and your situation is specific, a CPA should sign off before any of this moves from idea to action. To dig into the mechanics, pull up IRS Publication 526 on charitable contributions and read it alongside Schedule A — searching 'donor-advised fund deduction' on IRS.gov will get you there in under a minute.
