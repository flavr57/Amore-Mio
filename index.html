American Tower and Crown Castle: Landlords to the Wireless World
American Tower (ticker: AMT) and Crown Castle (CCI) are real estate investment trusts that own the physical cell towers wireless carriers need to transmit signals — they are landlords to AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile rather than to apartment tenants. Historically, tower REIT dividends have generally run in the 3–5% range, though that figure has shifted meaningfully with interest rate cycles, so any current number is worth checking fresh. The underlying mechanism is what makes them interesting: carriers sign long-term leases — typically five to ten years — with automatic annual rent escalators built in, creating predictable, inflation-linked income that compounds quietly over time. The main risk to watch is carrier consolidation; when T-Mobile absorbed Sprint, Crown Castle lost a major tenant and its growth story stalled for a period, a reminder that the landlord analogy has real limits when your entire tenant pool is three companies wide. For a deeper look, pull both companies' most recent 10-K filings to compare tower counts, lease expiration schedules, and international versus domestic exposure — then examine the Hoya Capital Cell Tower ETF (TOWR) to see how the sector behaves as a unit. Tomorrow we turn to a different kind of infrastructure landlord: Digital Realty and Equinix, the data center REITs that essentially house the internet itself.

Archaeologists Unearth a Clay Tablet Bearing the World's Oldest Peace Treaty
Researchers have unearthed a clay tablet inscribed with what is believed to be the world's oldest known peace treaty — an ancient text that opens with the declaration that the Sun God and the God of the Tempest have 'fixed for eternity' an accord between Egypt and the Hittite kingdom. The discovery offers a remarkable window into early diplomacy, suggesting that the human instinct to formalize peace in writing is at least as old as civilization itself. There is something quietly moving about finding, in a fragment of fired clay, the same yearning for permanence that still drives peace agreements today.

Neuroscientist Gregory Berns and his team at Emory University trained dogs to lie still in MRI machines and found that canine brains light up most powerfully in the reward center — the caudate nucleus — in response to their owner's scent, more than any other stimulus tested.
