When the Forest Itself Pays the Dividend
Weyerhaeuser (ticker: WY) is one of the largest timber REITs in the United States, owning and managing millions of acres of timberland — it earns income by harvesting and selling timber, then distributes a significant portion to shareholders, and has historically offered dividend yields that have run roughly in the 2–4% range depending on timber prices and the broader market cycle. Farmland vehicles like Farmland Partners Inc (FPI), a publicly traded farmland REIT, follow a similar logic: the land itself generates rent from tenant farmers, and that rent gets passed through to investors as regular income. The mechanism is elegantly simple — these are real assets producing real cash from biological and agricultural cycles that have little in common with the quarterly earnings theater of a software company. The honest trade-off is that timber and farmland REITs are sensitive to commodity prices, interest rates, and weather events in ways that more defensive sectors aren't, so the income stream can be lumpier than the headline yield implies. To dig deeper, it's worth pulling Weyerhaeuser's investor relations fact sheet alongside FPI's annual report to compare land base, payout history, and debt structure — then running a side-by-side with PotlatchDeltic (PCH) to see how different operators manage essentially the same underlying asset class. Tomorrow we turn to BND and AGG — the two total bond market funds that quietly anchor more portfolios than almost anything else.

Stone Age Arrowheads Emerge from Norwegian Glaciers After 5,000 Years
Scientists on the world's largest glacial archaeology expedition have recovered 48 Stone Age arrowheads and wooden shafts from melting glaciers in southern Norway — objects preserved for millennia by ice and now surfacing as that ice retreats. The finds are part of a 20-year survey that has steadily rewritten our understanding of how ancient peoples hunted and traveled through high mountain terrain. There is a bittersweet quality to the science: every artifact is a gift from the deep past, and every gift is made possible by loss.

The Secret to Conservation Success? Ask the People Who Live There
A sweeping new analysis of conservation outcomes around the world has identified a remarkably consistent pattern: the projects that succeed in protecting endangered species and ecosystems are almost always ones where local communities have genuine voice and involvement in the decisions. The researchers found this held across continents and ecosystems — from tropical forests to coral reefs — suggesting that top-down mandates, however well-funded, rarely match the durability of locally owned stewardship. It turns out the people closest to the land have always known something worth listening to.
