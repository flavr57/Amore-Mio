The Big BDC Lenders: How Ares Capital and Hercules Put Money to Work
Ares Capital (ARCC) and Hercules Capital are two of the largest Business Development Companies — publicly traded firms that lend to and invest in middle-market businesses that typically can't access traditional bank financing. ARCC, the largest BDC by assets, has historically distributed yields in roughly the 8–11% range; Hercules, which focuses specifically on venture-backed technology and life-sciences companies, has run in a similar ballpark — both figures are approximate and shift with rate cycles and credit conditions. The income mechanism is structural: BDCs are required by law to distribute at least 90% of their taxable income to shareholders, and because most of their loans carry floating rates, their earnings tend to rise alongside interest rates. The honest trade-off is credit risk — middle-market borrowers are more vulnerable than investment-grade issuers in a downturn, so dividend cuts and net-asset-value erosion are genuinely possible, not just theoretical. Worth pulling: ARCC's and Hercules's most recent 10-K filings and quarterly investor presentations, where you can compare non-accrual rates, portfolio composition by industry, and leverage ratios — these are the numbers that tell you whether the yield is being earned sustainably or borrowed from future capital. Butterfly Wings Are Showing Scientists a Cleaner Way to Make Paint
Researchers studying the iridescent scales of butterfly wings are exploring structural color — color that comes from light interference rather than chemical pigments — which could yield paints and coatings free of the toxic compounds currently used in manufacturing. Butterflies produce their vivid blues and greens without any dye at all, using nano-scale architecture that bends light itself. If the science scales, it could quietly eliminate a surprising source of industrial pollution from something as ordinary as a painted wall.

November Is the Sweet Spot for Coaxing Bougainvillea Into Full Bloom
As Hermosa Beach settles into its sunniest autumn days, bougainvillea enters one of its most responsive windows — slightly cooler nights combined with reduced watering signal the plant to push out those brilliant papery bracts. The key is a mild stress technique: hold back irrigation for four to six weeks until you see the first flush of color, then resume normal watering to sustain the display. Full sun and lean soil are the bougainvillea's native language; too much shade or nitrogen-rich fertilizer will give you lush green and very little of that spectacular magenta.

As days shorten even in Southern California, phalaenopsis orchids appreciate being moved closer to a bright east- or south-facing window, where they receive indirect light without searing midday rays.
