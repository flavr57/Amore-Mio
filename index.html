The Numbers That Actually Tell You What a REIT Earns
When you read a real estate investment trust's income statement, net earnings will almost always look artificially low — sometimes even negative — because REITs must depreciate their properties on paper even as those properties often appreciate in the real world. FFO (Funds From Operations) corrects for this by adding depreciation back to net income, giving a far more accurate picture of the cash the business is actually generating; AFFO (Adjusted Funds From Operations) goes one step further by also subtracting the recurring capital expenditures needed to keep the portfolio in good working order. Equity REITs have historically offered dividend yields somewhere in a loose 4–6% range in normal rate environments, though that range drifts considerably with interest rates and property sector, and past distributions say nothing guaranteed about future ones. The mechanism is baked into REIT law itself: these trusts must distribute at least 90% of their taxable income to shareholders to maintain their tax-favored status, and FFO is the number that most honestly reflects how much cash is available to sustain those payouts. The honest watch-out is that AFFO is not a standardized metric — each company defines it slightly differently, so comparing two REITs on AFFO alone requires reading the footnotes carefully to make sure you're comparing like with like. A concrete thread to pull: find the quarterly supplemental reports published by Realty Income (ticker: O) and Public Storage, both of which include detailed FFO and AFFO breakdowns, then watch how the dividend coverage ratio changes depending on whether you use reported earnings or AFFO. Tomorrow we turn to mortgage REITs like AGNC and Annaly — a completely different structure from equity REITs, with yields that can look almost startling, and reasons behind those numbers that are very much worth understanding clearly.

Virginia Will Now Automatically Seal Thousands of Minor Criminal Records
Virginia's new Clean Slate law took effect last week, meaning thousands of misdemeanor convictions will be automatically sealed — without requiring residents to hire a lawyer, navigate paperwork, or even know the relief was available to them. The law recognizes that minor past offenses can follow people for decades, limiting housing, employment, and opportunity long after they've moved on with their lives. It's a meaningful shift in how a state extends second chances: quietly, automatically, and without asking anyone to beg for grace.

October Warmth Is Your Signal to Start Pushing Orchids Toward Next Bloom
With daytime temperatures still climbing into the high eighties, this week is an ideal moment to give your phalaenopsis orchids the one cue they need to form a new flower spike: a temperature differential between day and night, ideally 10–15°F cooler after dark, which these plants have evolved to read as the trigger for setting buds. If your orchid has been in a west-facing window all summer, ease it toward an east-facing spot now — the gentler morning light combined with the cooling evenings sends exactly the right signal without stressing the roots. Hold back on fertilizer for the next few weeks and let the plant channel its energy into spike initiation rather than leafy growth.
