The Bond Market in One Fund: What BND and AGG Actually Do
BND (Vanguard Total Bond Market ETF) and AGG (iShares Core U.S. Aggregate Bond Index ETF) are the two flagship funds that track the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index — a sprawling collection of thousands of investment-grade US bonds, including Treasuries, mortgage-backed securities, and investment-grade corporates, all in a single ticker. In terms of what they have historically paid, yields have generally run somewhere in the 2–5% range depending on the interest rate environment, with the pendulum pushing toward the higher end in recent years as rates have climbed. The income comes from coupon payments collected across all those underlying bonds, pooled and passed through to shareholders as monthly distributions — the fund essentially does the bond-ladder work for you at scale. The honest trade-off is duration risk: because these funds hold bonds across a range of maturities, rising interest rates push the fund's price down, sometimes meaningfully, even as the income holds steady. When you are ready to dig deeper, pull the fund fact sheets directly from Vanguard and iShares to compare current yield-to-maturity, average duration, and expense ratios, then run the same screen on SCHZ (Schwab's equivalent) to see how the three stack up before deciding which wrapper fits your account. Tomorrow we step into LQD and VCIT — the investment-grade corporate bond funds that aim for a bit more yield in exchange for a bit more credit exposure.
