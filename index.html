The Bucket System: Letting Time Do the Heavy Lifting
The bucket approach divides your savings into distinct pools based on when you will actually need the money — typically a short-term spending bucket of one to two years of living expenses held in cash or near-cash instruments, a medium-term buffer bucket in bonds or CDs, and a long-term growth bucket in equities or REITs. The spending bucket, when parked in high-yield savings or short-term Treasuries, has historically run in the 4–5% range in higher-rate environments, while a diversified equity bucket has historically returned somewhere in the 7–10% annualized range over long periods — though both figures are loose historical patterns, not guarantees of any kind. The mechanism that makes it work is as psychological as it is mechanical: keeping living expenses in a separate, stable bucket means you are never forced to sell growth assets during a market downturn just to cover next month's bills. The honest trade-off is that buckets require active maintenance — they need to be replenished as the spending bucket depletes, and that discipline is easy to let slide. For a named, well-documented framework to pull apart, search Christine Benz's bucket portfolio research at Morningstar, or look up Vanguard's and Fidelity's published model bucket portfolios to see how different firms size and rebalance between buckets over time. Tomorrow we look at high-yield savings accounts at online banks — a natural home for that short-term spending bucket, and quite a different animal from what your brick-and-mortar branch is likely offering you.

Amsterdam's Schiphol Airport became an unlikely conservation battleground when Dutch authorities seized 26 Vallarta mud turtles from a single passenger's luggage bound for Hong Kong. Bougainvillea in Winter: The Quiet Season That Makes the Summer Show
Bougainvillea's famously extravagant flowering is built on a cool, dry, and somewhat stressful winter — the plant blooms most lavishly when it has been made to work for it. This is the season to ease off watering significantly, letting the top two inches of soil dry completely between waterings, and to hold fertilizer entirely until late February. A bougainvillea that winters slightly lean and slightly cool will produce far more vivid color come spring than one that was coddled straight through the cold months.
