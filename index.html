The Extra Yield in Your Bond Portfolio: HYG, JNK, and Why
HYG (iShares iBoxx $ High Yield Corporate Bond ETF) and JNK (SPDR Bloomberg High Yield Bond ETF) are two of the most widely traded funds that track the high-yield — or 'junk bond' — market, which is debt issued by companies whose credit ratings fall below investment grade. Historically, these funds have offered yields somewhere in the 5–9% range, though that spread widens and narrows considerably with economic conditions and where we are in the credit cycle. The extra income is the price of credit risk: investors are compensated for the higher probability that some issuers may default, miss a payment, or be downgraded further. The honest trade-off is that high-yield bonds behave more like equities than Treasuries during a market downturn — prices can fall sharply when investors get nervous about the economy, often exactly when you'd least want them to. A productive next step is to pull the fund fact sheets for both HYG and JNK directly from iShares and State Street, compare their expense ratios, average credit quality distributions, and durations, then run a screen on Morningstar to see how each behaved during the 2008 and 2020 credit crunches. Tomorrow: a quieter cousin in the bond world — target-maturity ETFs from iShares and Invesco that actually wind down on a fixed date, almost like holding a bond directly.

Your Orchid Wants Less Water and a Chilly Night — Give It Both
Phalaenopsis orchids are killed far more often by overwatering than by anything else, and October is an ideal time to reset: water at most once a week, let the roots go nearly dry between drinks, and make sure the pot drains fully each time. The cooling nights in Southern California right now are actually a gift — that 10–15 degree temperature drop between day and night is precisely what triggers a new flower spike in Phalaenopsis. If yours hasn't rebloomed in over a year, move it to a spot near a window where it can feel that overnight chill, and give it a few weeks to respond.
