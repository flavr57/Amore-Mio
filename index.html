Your Kids' Investment Income Might Be Taxed at Your Rate
The kiddie tax — formally Section 1(g) of the Internal Revenue Code — applies to a child's unearned income above a small annual threshold: rather than being taxed at the child's own lower rate, that investment income gets taxed at the parents' marginal rate instead. The threshold where the parent's rate kicks in has historically landed in the low-to-mid thousands of dollars of unearned income, so a teen with even a modest custodial brokerage account can hit it; when a parent is in the 22–32% range, the difference can swing several hundred dollars a year. Congress designed this rule specifically to close the loophole of parking dividend-paying stocks or bond funds in a child's name to harvest their lower bracket — essentially saying that a family's investment returns are the family's, regardless of whose Social Security number appears on the 1099. The detail most people miss: full-time students can remain subject to the kiddie tax all the way through age 23, so your college-bound 18-year-old may not age out of this the moment they walk across the graduation stage. These rules have shifted before — the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act rewrote the calculation method, then Congress partially reversed that change — so take this as a research map rather than a how-to guide, confirm current thresholds and age rules with a CPA before adjusting any accounts. The place to dig is IRS Form 8615, 'Tax for Certain Children Who Have Unearned Income,' read alongside Publication 929; searching the exact phrase 'Form 8615 instructions' pulls up the current IRS worksheet and eligibility rules in one click. October Is the Right Moment to Set Your Orchid Up for Spring Blooms
As light quality shifts and nights cool in October, Phalaenopsis orchids benefit from being moved to a bright, indirect-light spot where overnight temperatures dip noticeably — that 10 to 15 degree differential between day and night is precisely what triggers a bloom spike come spring. Resist the urge to water more generously as growth slows; soggy roots sitting in cooler soil is the most reliable way to lose an otherwise thriving plant. A monthly feed at quarter-strength with a balanced orchid fertilizer will hold the roots and foliage in good shape through winter without pushing unwanted leafy growth at the expense of flowers.
