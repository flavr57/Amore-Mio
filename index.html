Exxon and Chevron: How Oil Giants Engineer a Dividend That Endures
Exxon Mobil and Chevron are the two largest US-based integrated oil majors — meaning they earn across the full chain from drilling and production through refining and distribution, rather than at just one point in the commodity cycle. Both have historically run dividend yields in the rough neighborhood of 3–5%, though that figure shifts with the share price and has stretched higher in downturns when the payout held steady but the stock slid. The mechanism that keeps those checks arriving through low oil prices is integration plus an explicit cultural commitment to the dividend: both companies have repeatedly drawn on balance-sheet reserves and asset disposals rather than cut, treating the payout as nearly inviolable. The honest trade-off is that defending a dividend through a sustained commodity slump means debt rises, and that leverage eventually limits flexibility — worth tracking closely on their quarterly filings. A concrete research thread: pull Chevron's investor relations page and look at its long-run dividend-per-share growth chart — Chevron has maintained Dividend Aristocrat status (25+ consecutive years of growth), and comparing its track record with Exxon's more complicated recent history is a sharp study in how two similar businesses can make different choices under pressure. September's Secret: Why This Month Is Bougainvillea's Hidden Best Friend
As the summer heat softens in Southern California, bougainvillea enters one of its sweetest windows — nights cool enough to trigger a final brilliant flush of color, days still warm enough to sustain it. This is the moment to ease off heavy watering deliberately: bougainvillea blooms most vigorously when slightly stressed, and an over-watered plant in September will push green growth at the expense of those vivid papery bracts. Resist the nitrogen fertilizer now, let the soil dry between waterings, and a light trim after this cycle closes will set up an even stronger show come spring.
