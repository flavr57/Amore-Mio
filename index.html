The Dividend Growth Bet: Why VIG and DGRO Favor the Rising Tide
VIG (Vanguard Dividend Appreciation ETF) and DGRO (iShares Core Dividend Growth ETF) are index funds built around a specific conviction: instead of chasing the highest current yield, they hold companies with a track record of consistently growing their dividends year over year. Historically, both have delivered yields in roughly the 1.5–2.5% range — modest by income-fund standards — but their appeal lies in the compounding trajectory rather than the immediate payout. The mechanism is straightforward: companies that raise dividends reliably tend to be financially healthy businesses with genuine pricing power, which means the income stream itself grows over time, often outpacing inflation in a way a fixed-rate bond cannot. The honest trade-off is patience — if you need meaningful income today, dividend growth funds will feel underwhelming compared to high-yield alternatives, and neither fund is insulated from equity market drawdowns when they come. A concrete next step is to pull each fund's prospectus and compare their inclusion criteria side by side: VIG requires ten-plus consecutive years of dividend increases, while DGRO casts a somewhat wider net, and seeing exactly which companies pass each screen tells you a great deal about what you're actually buying. Bougainvillea's Last Summer Push: Coax One More Flush Before Fall
September is bougainvillea's final opportunity for a vivid late-season bloom in Southern California, and the secret is a little strategic stress. Ease back on watering now: drought stress triggers those brilliant papery bracts to color up, while overwatering pushes the plant back toward leafy growth and away from the magentas and purples you want. A light application of a low-nitrogen, high-phosphorus fertilizer gives the roots a gentle nudge toward flowering rather than foliage — and the results, a few weeks out, are worth the restraint.

Orchid Light Check: Shorter Days Mean It's Time to Move the Pot
September brings a subtle but meaningful shift in sun angle that catches orchid growers by surprise each year — the light that was ideal on an east-facing sill in July may now be too dim or too raking depending on your windows. Phalaenopsis orchids want bright, indirect light year-round, so as the sun drops lower in the sky, move them closer to the glass without letting a leaf touch the pane. A simple test: if your hand casts a faint, soft shadow on the leaf at midday, the light level is about right.
