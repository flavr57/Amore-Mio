What Hides Inside Emerging Market Bond Funds: The Currency Question
Emerging market bond funds pool together debt issued by governments and corporations in developing economies — Brazil, Indonesia, South Africa, Mexico — and package it into a vehicle investors can buy through an ordinary brokerage account. Funds in this category have historically paid somewhere in the 4–7% yield range, though that figure moves around considerably depending on market conditions and which countries make up the portfolio. The income comes from the interest those bonds pay; the appeal is structural — developing economies typically need to offer richer yields to attract capital, so the coupon is meaningfully higher than what you'd find on U.S. Treasuries or investment-grade European debt. The key question to sit with is whether a given fund holds bonds denominated in U.S. dollars or in local currencies: dollar-denominated funds like the iShares J.P. Morgan USD Emerging Markets Bond ETF (EMB) insulate you from exchange-rate swings, while local-currency funds like the VanEck J.P. Morgan EM Local Currency Bond ETF (EMLC) let you participate in — or get burned by — how those currencies move against the dollar. A good place to start: compare the prospectus and fact sheets for both EMB and EMLC side by side, looking at historical volatility, country weights, and yield-to-maturity to see how differently the two have behaved during periods of dollar strength. ETH Zurich Finds a Low-Energy Way to Recycle Acrylic Glass Completely
Researchers at ETH Zurich have developed a practical method that breaks acrylic glass — the material behind Plexiglas — almost entirely back down into its original molecular building blocks, ready to become brand-new acrylic with no loss in quality. The process uses remarkably little energy compared to conventional recycling, and sidesteps the usual problem where repeated processing degrades the material over time. It's the kind of chemistry breakthrough that makes the circular economy feel less like an aspiration and more like an engineering problem that's quietly getting solved.

Orchids in October: Coaxing the Next Bloom Spike With Cooler Nights
As fall arrives — even the gentle version Hermosa Beach offers — orchids respond to the slight drop in nighttime temperatures by beginning to prepare for their next bloom cycle, and this is the moment to work with that signal rather than against it. Ease off fertilizer, let the potting mix dry out a little more between waterings, and move the plant somewhere it can feel that 10-to-15-degree overnight temperature differential that triggers spike formation. The rest period feels like neglect, but it's the setup: six to eight weeks of this, and a new spike is usually on its way.

Bougainvillea's Secret to Vivid Color: Stress It, Then Stand Back
Bougainvillea's spectacular bracts — those vivid pinks, reds, and corals that stop traffic — are a stress response, not a sign of coddled care: the plant blooms most dramatically when it's slightly root-bound, running a little dry, and getting full sun for the better part of the day. Too much water or too generous a nitrogen fertilizer pushes it into lush green growth at the expense of color, so resist the urge to fuss. In late October, ease off watering noticeably and let it feel the dryness — you're setting up a spring flush of color that makes the patience more than worthwhile.
